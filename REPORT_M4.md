# REPORT_M4.md — Query optimizer measurements

Every number here was produced by `python -m bench.m4_measure` and is in `results/m4/`.

Each query goes the whole way: SQL → Module 3 → logical plan → Module 4 → physical plan →
Module 2 → rows. Page I/O is what the executor's disk manager counted. B = 8 unless stated.

## 0. Assumptions: the schemas, the data and the workload

No dataset and no queries were published with the specification, so both are mine. Everything
below is generated by `bench/m4_measure.py` from one fixed seed, so the database and every number
in this report are the same on every run. The database is a small order system. It was built to
have what the specification says its dataset has, strongly correlated columns, and to give the
optimizer something to decide: tables of different sizes, a skewed column, selective and
unselective predicates.

### Schemas

| Table | Columns | Rows | Data pages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer | cid INT, name VARCHAR(16), city VARCHAR(12), country VARCHAR(10), segment INT, note VARCHAR(60) | 300 | 7 |
| Orders | oid INT, cid INT, odate INT, shipdate INT, status INT, total INT, note VARCHAR(60) | 2,000 | 40 |
| Item | oid INT, lineno INT, pid INT, qty INT, price INT, note VARCHAR(40) | 3,000 | 42 |
| Product | pid INT, pname VARCHAR(20), category INT, brand INT, weight INT, note VARCHAR(60) | 200 | 5 |
| Supplier | sid INT, sname VARCHAR(16), country VARCHAR(10) | 30 | 1 |
| Supply | pid INT, sid INT, cost INT | 500 | 2 |

Every table is analyzed with the default 20 buckets before anything is planned.

### Sample data

| Table | How its rows are made |
|---|---|
| Customer | cid 0 to 299. city is one of 30, drawn uniformly. **country is determined by city**: six countries, five cities each. segment is uniform on 0 to 4. |
| Orders | oid 0 to 1,999. cid uniform on 0 to 299. odate uniform on 1 to 1,000. **shipdate is odate plus 1 to 5.** status is 0 for 60 percent of orders and uniform on 1 to 4 for the rest. total uniform on 1 to 5,000. |
| Item | oid uniform on 0 to 1,999. lineno cycles 0 to 4. pid is **skewed**: product p is drawn with weight 1 / (p + 1), so a few products hold most of the items. qty 1 to 10, price 1 to 499. |
| Product | pid 0 to 199. brand is pid mod 20. **category is determined by brand**: brand divided by 4, so five categories of four brands. weight 1 to 299. |
| Supplier | sid 0 to 29, country cycles through the six. |
| Supply | 500 rows, pid uniform on 0 to 199, sid uniform on 0 to 29, cost 1 to 99. |

The `note` columns are 20 to 60 characters of padding. They are there so that the tables have
enough pages for page I/O to mean something while the row counts stay small.

The three correlated pairs are the ones in bold: `Customer.city` → `Customer.country`,
`Orders.odate` → `Orders.shipdate`, `Product.brand` → `Product.category`. Section 4 uses the first
two.

Four different causes, and only one of them is the estimator being wrong about the data.

- **Q11, 19.9: not an estimation error at all.** The sort is under `LIMIT 10`. It was estimated
  to produce 199 rows and would have, but the limit stopped asking after 10, so the executor
  counted 10. The estimate describes what the operator can produce, the count what it was asked
  for.
- **Q15, 13.5, and Q16, 8.5: the join formula's assumption.** n(R) × n(S) / max(ndv, ndv)
  assumes every value on one side finds partners on the other. In Q15 the two sides are orders
  from the first 14 days and from the last 14: 27 on each side, estimated to share customers at
  the rate all orders do. In Q16 one side is a filter on `COUNT(*)`, a column with no statistics,
  which gets the default of 1/3.
- **Q19, 4.5, and Q20, 5.1: the correlated pairs.** `city = 'Lima' AND country = 'Peru'` and
  `odate > 800 AND shipdate > 800`. Independence multiplies two selectivities where the second
  predicate removes nothing. Section 4 is about these. Note how the error travels: Q20's filter
  is off by 5.1, and so is every node above it.
- **Q18, 4.1: skew meeting a join.** `brand = 7` selects 10 of 200 products, estimated exactly.
  But a few products account for most items, and the formula spreads items evenly over products.

### Predicted cost against actual I/O

The last two columns of the per-query table. Fourteen of twenty are exact and fifteen are within
ten percent, because most of these plans are scans plus joins of small projected inputs.
Four misses are under-predictions: Q12 (184 predicted, 269 done), Q6, Q13 and Q20, all plans with
a nested loop join whose outer is another join or whose inner was underestimated. Section 4
takes one apart. The fifth, Q8, is over-predicted, 61 against 53: a group_by over a join, where
the model charges for reading the join's result and the executor pipelines it (DESIGN_M4.md
section 4).

**Over the workload the optimizer does 6.5 times less I/O: 1,519 pages against 9,891.** Per query
the ratio runs from 1.00 to 19.

- **Ratio 1.00: six queries** (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q10, Q11, Q17), all without a join. A scan costs its
  pages whatever is done above it. There is nothing to choose.
- **Ratio 1.5 to 4.6: ten queries, two-table joins and grouping.** The gain is projection: the
  unoptimized plan drags the padding column through the join, or sorts full-width rows to group
  them (Q7 and Q9: 128 against 40).
- **Ratio 8 to 19: Q6, Q8, Q13, Q14.** Unoptimized, a join's outer is a full-width table or the
  full-width result of the previous join, many blocks long, and the inner is read once per
  block. Optimized, selections and projections have shrunk every input to a page or two before
  any join starts.

The comparisons column is the CPU side: 46 million against 10 million. Q5, the comma join, goes
from 1,200,000 to 13,025: unoptimized it is a cross product of 300 customers and 2,000 orders,
filtered afterwards.

### Does B change the plan

| query | B = 4 | B = 8 | B = 64 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q6 | hash + nested_loop | nested_loop + nested_loop | nested_loop + nested_loop |
| Q12 | nested_loop + hash + nested_loop | nested_loop + nested_loop + nested_loop | nested_loop + nested_loop + nested_loop |

Two queries change algorithm between B = 4 and B = 8. At B = 4 a block is two pages, so a nested
loop over a 33-page outer would read its inner 17 times, and a hash join is cheaper; at B = 8 the
block is six pages and the nested loop wins. For the other queries the join inputs are so small
after projection that everything fits in one block at any B.

## 3. Which rule mattered

One rule off at a time, the rest on, the whole workload.

| rule | what it does | I/O with all rules | I/O without this rule | ratio | queries whose I/O changed | largest changes | comparisons, ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | split conjunctions | 1519 | 2355 | 1.55 | 6 | Q6 +355, Q13 +315, Q14 +137, Q15 +22 | 3.38 |
| 2 | push selects below joins | 1519 | 2476 | 1.63 | 10 | Q6 +355, Q13 +315, Q14 +137, Q20 +108 | 3.35 |
| 3 | cross + select = join | 1519 | 1519 | 1.00 | 0 | - | 1.00 |
| 4 | push selects through rename, project, union | 1519 | 1519 | 1.00 | 0 | - | 1.00 |
| 5 | push selects below group_by | 1519 | 1519 | 1.00 | 0 | - | 1.00 |
| 6 | push projections down | 1519 | 2640 | 1.74 | 9 | Q12 +658, Q13 +177, Q14 +149, Q8 +114 | 0.42 |
| 7 | tidy up | 1519 | 1519 | 1.00 | 0 | - | 1.00 |

**Three rules carry all of it: 6, 2 and 1.**

- **Rule 6, projections: 1.74 times the I/O without it.** The largest, on this data, because of
  how the data is shaped: every big table has a wide padding column that few queries want.
  Without rule 6 that column rides through every join, into every spool, block and partition.
  The comparisons column says 0.42: with wide inputs the optimizer switches to hash joins, which
  compare far less, and pays for it in pages.
- **Rule 2, pushing selections below joins: 1.63.** Ten queries.
- **Rule 1, splitting conjunctions: 1.55.** It does nothing by itself. It is worth exactly what
  rule 2 can no longer do when a WHERE clause arrives as one piece: a conjunction that mentions
  two tables cannot be pushed to either.

**Four rules are worth nothing in page I/O here, each for its own reason.**

- **Rule 3, cross plus select into join: 1.00.** The only comma join is Q5. With rule 3 off it
  stays a cross product with a filter above, but rule 2 has already cut both sides to a page
  each, and a nested loop over two one-page inputs costs the same I/O with or without a
  condition. On a comma join whose inputs did *not* shrink, rule 3 would be the difference
  between a join and a product; this workload does not contain one, because the unoptimized
  baseline would not finish.
- **Rule 4, through rename, projection and union, and rule 5, below group_by: 1.00.** They
  fire, in Q9, Q16 and Q17, and the plans change. But a filter is pipelined and costs no I/O
  wherever it sits; moving it only pays when it shrinks something that is then written or read
  again, and here the group_by tables and unions fit in memory either way.
- **Rule 7, tidying: 1.00**, as it should be. It changes what explain prints.

So on this data "push projections, push selections" is the whole benefit of rewriting, and the
finer rules are about plans this workload is too small to need. I would not conclude they are
useless. I would conclude that an I/O-only cost model cannot see what a pipelined filter saves.

## 4. Where it breaks

### The assumption, in isolation

| predicates | rows passing the first | rows passing the second | estimated rows passing both | actual rows passing both | q-error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| odate > 800 AND shipdate > 800 | 392 | 398 | 77 | 392 | 5.1 |
| city = 'Lima' AND country = 'Peru' | 9 | 49 | 2 | 9 | 4.5 |
| odate < 100 AND shipdate > 900 | 198 | 181 | 19 | 0 | 19.0 |

For the first two the second predicate removes nothing the first had not: an order placed after
day 800 ships after day 800; a customer in Lima is in Peru. The truth is the smaller of the two
counts. The estimate multiplies the two fractions, and is too low by a factor of 5.1 and 4.5.
The third is the same dependence the other way: an order placed before day 100 cannot ship after
day 900. The truth is 0 and the estimate is 19.

number (`--assume`). When the two plans differ, both are run.

**What happened.** Believing Orders shrinks to 494 rows, 8 pages, the optimizer joined Item to
Orders first (146 estimated, against 206 for the other order) and Product last. With the true 992
rows, 15 pages, joining Item to Product first and Orders last is cheaper, because the wide
Orders rows are then read as an inner input instead of being carried through a second join.

**How the two plans really ran.** In the chosen plan the Item–Orders join read its inner, the
15-page spool of filtered orders, 33 times: 495 page reads for a join the optimizer predicted at
64. In the informed plan the same 15 pages were read 13 times: 195.

**Two errors are in that 495, and only one is the correlation.** The inner is 15 pages where 8
were expected: that is independence. But the number of passes, 33 where 7 were expected, is the
cost model: it gives every join all B frames, and the executor splits them between two joins
that run at the same time, which left this one a block of a single frame (DESIGN_M4.md section
4). The correlation chose the plan; the frame split made the plan it chose expensive. Had the
estimate been right the optimizer would have picked the other order, which suffers less from the
split because its lower join has a one-page inner.

**A larger error that cost nothing.** `odate > 800 AND shipdate > 800` is off by 5.1, not 2.0, and
appears nowhere in the table: at these sizes no plan changed when the optimizer was told the
truth. Whether a wrong estimate matters depends on whether a decision sits near it. At threshold
500 the two join orders were 146 against 206 apart, close enough for a factor of two to flip.

### What statistic would have prevented it

Something about the two columns *together*. Single-column statistics cannot hold it, however
many buckets they have.

- **The number of distinct (city, country) pairs.** It is 30, the same as the number of distinct
  cities. That equality says country is determined by city, and then sel(city = x AND country = y)
  is sel(city = x), not the product. ANALYZE could get it from one more sort, on the pair.
- **For the dates, a two-dimensional histogram on (odate, shipdate)**, or more cheaply a
  histogram of the difference shipdate − odate, which has five values. Either shows that all the
  rows lie in a narrow band along the diagonal, and a query's rectangle can be intersected with
  the band instead of with two independent margins.
- **Short of new statistics, a cautious rule:** for a conjunction on columns known to be
  correlated, use the smaller selectivity instead of the product. It is exact for a functional
  dependency and an upper bound otherwise.

Which pairs to keep joint statistics for is the hard part: there are too many pairs to keep them
all. The honest version is that someone declares them, or the optimizer notices a q-error like
the ones in section 1 and asks.

## 6. What I would change

1. **Tell the cost model how the executor divides frames.** It is the largest error in section
   4 and it has nothing to do with statistics. For a left-deep chain the split is known from the
   depth alone: the top join has B, the one below it about half.
2. **Joint statistics for declared column pairs**, as in section 4.
3. **Join estimates that know about skew.** n(R) n(S) / max(ndv) is exact for a key and wrong for
   Q18, where a few values hold most of the rows. The histograms of the two join columns are
   already there and could be matched bucket by bucket.
4. **A cost for pipelined work.** Section 3 could not see what rules 3, 4 and 5 save because a
   filter costs nothing in page I/O.

## 7. Sources

- Selinger, Astrahan, Chamberlin, Lorie and Price, *Access Path Selection in a Relational
  Database Management System*, SIGMOD 1979. The enumeration is theirs: dynamic programming over
  subsets, left-deep trees, cross products put off until nothing else is possible. So are the
  default selectivities used when there are no statistics, 1/10 for an equality and 1/3 for a
  range.
- Leis, Gubichev, Mirchev, Boncz, Kemper and Neumann, *How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?*,
  VLDB 2015. Their finding that cardinality errors do more harm than cost model errors, and that
  errors grow up the plan, is why section 1 reports q-error per node and not per query, and why
  the case in section 4 was taken apart into its two errors instead of being reported as one
  number. In that case the cost model's blind spot was the larger of the two, which is not what
  they found in general, and the reason is specific: an executor that splits a small B.
